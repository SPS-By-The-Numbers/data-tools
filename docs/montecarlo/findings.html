What Would It Do to the Buses?

Monte Carlo estimates of how fourteen policy scenarios — walk-zone changes, restored high-school service, school closures, and option-school conversions — would change Seattle Public Schools yellow-bus ridership, fleet size, and state transportation funding.

Generated 2026-06-11 · 200 paired draws per scenario (seed 20260610) · baseline school year 2024-25 · methods: OVERVIEW.md

§1How to read this

Every number is a change from the 2024-25 baseline (10,008.5 basic rides/day, 202 routes, ≈180 buses on the modeled basic+gifted programs — the district’s full fleet is ≈382, the rest serving special-ed and other programs outside this model — and $36.7M state reimbursement). “Rides per day” counts boardings the way the state does: morning and afternoon each count once, so a student riding both ways counts as 2. Brackets are 95% intervals over a few hundred simulated worlds that vary where students live (census uncertainty) and how readily families use the bus (behavioral-parameter uncertainty) — they are not year-to-year noise. Where a scenario changes who is bus-eligible, uptake = Δrides ÷ Δeligible: the expected rides/day per newly-eligible student. Its theoretical max is 2 (a both-ways rider counts twice), so e.g. 0.60 means somewhere between 30% of the new students riding both ways and 60% riding one way.

The one-sentence summary: expanding service earns the district money — added riders raise the state’s funding formula faster than they raise costs, and middle/high-school routes reuse buses the elementary shift already pays for — while school closures lose state funding (≈$550k per closed served school) far faster than they save bus costs.

§5The money: funding formula vs bus costs

Washington reimburses districts through the STARS “Expected Allocation” formula. It pays for more riders, more served schools, and longer average routes — at the current margin roughly $2,650 per daily boarding-count per year and roughly $550k per served school.

§7Key findings

  1. Service expansion pays for itself under the funding formula. Shrinking middle-school walk zones to 1 mile adds ≈2,070 rides/day, zero buses, and ≈+$4.3M/yr net. Restoring high-school yellow bus adds ≈+$4.5M/yr net even at heavily discounted HS ridership — and nearly all HS sites already receive special-ed service, so only one new destination (Nova) enters the funding formula.
  2. Closures lose state funding far faster than they save bus money. The 21-school Option A plan trims ridership slightly (−147 rides/day — displaced students scatter to nearby existing choices) and saves ≈$1.1M of bus cost, but forfeits −$10.3M/yr of state funding (every closed school is a served destination, even where the model carries no rides) — net ≈ −$9.2M/yr, an offset of roughly a third of its claimed $31.5M building savings. Option B nets ≈ −$8.2M/yr.
  3. Designated consolidations behave differently from free choice. When students are assigned to one named receiver (fab-4), ex-walkers usually land outside its walk zone and ridership rises (e.g. Sanislo has ≈0 riders today, yet closing it into Highland Park adds ~74 rides/day). Under the default everywhere-rule they spread to their area’s existing — mostly nearby — choices instead: closing Sacajawea sends a third of its students to Hazel Wolf K-8, the area’s most popular alternative, and district rides fall 29/day.
  4. The bell-shift structure makes middle/high-school service nearly free in fleet terms while the elementary shift remains larger — the cheapest capacity in the system is on the MS/HS shift.
  5. Option-school conversions are fiscally a wash (net ≈ $0.0M/yr): converting the five Option-A option schools cuts ≈170 rides/day and ≈3 buses as big lottery ridersheds become walker populations, but the rider revenue lost in the funding formula (≈$0.5M) almost exactly offsets the bus savings.
  6. Closures shift riders toward higher-poverty schools — even where the district total falls, the rider gains concentrate in the highest-low-income tercile (Option A: +525 there vs −901 in the middle tercile; see per-run equity cuts in the full reports).
Methods & caveats
  1. Full technical documentation, calibration and validation: OVERVIEW.md. Per-scenario detail (per-school movers, distance distributions, equity cuts): simulate/<scenario>/report.txt.
  2. High-school ridership is an uncalibrated assumption (SPS has never run HS yellow bus in the data): modeled as middle-school behavior ×0.7 for independent transit travel, with 11th–12th graders additionally ×0.5 for cars. Both factors are sampled, which is why HS intervals are wide.
  3. Funding deltas assume the EXAL formula’s coefficients hold and the prior-year cap never binds (true for every scenario shown), and apply modeled deltas to the official SY2024-25 formula inputs.
  4. Closure scenarios use the district’s named receivers where published (fab-4, Thurgood Marshall’s HCC); elsewhere, displaced students redistribute to any open school pro-rata to their residence area’s existing draw (revealed choice, dominated by distance; no enrollment caps) — not official boundary redraws. Building-operations savings are out of scope.
  5. Bus cost = Δfleet × the all-in vendor average ($148.9k/bus-yr) plus an assumed 2.0 overage driver-hours/route/day ($61.50/hr × 175 days ≈ $21.5k/route-yr) for routes riding existing buses. The hours figure is a judgment call — the district’s ~$9.8M/yr “extras” bucket can’t be broken down — so the table shows the fleet-only cost alongside as the lower bound.
