What Would It Do to the Buses?

Monte Carlo estimates of how eleven policy scenarios — walk-zone changes, restored high-school service, school closures, and option-school conversions — would change Seattle Public Schools yellow-bus ridership, fleet size, and state transportation funding.

Generated 2026-06-10 · 200 paired draws per scenario (seed 20260610) · baseline school year 2024-25 · methods: OVERVIEW.md

§1How to read this

Every number is a change from the 2024-25 baseline (10,008.5 basic rides/day, 202 routes, ≈180 buses, $36.7M state reimbursement). “Rides per day” counts boardings the way the state does: morning and afternoon each count once, so a student riding both ways counts as 2. Brackets are 95% intervals over a few hundred simulated worlds that vary where students live (census uncertainty) and how readily families use the bus (behavioral-parameter uncertainty) — they are not year-to-year noise.

The one-sentence summary: expanding service earns the district money — added riders raise the state’s funding formula faster than they raise costs, and middle/high-school routes reuse buses the elementary shift already pays for — while school closures lose transportation money from both directions at once.

§4The money: funding formula vs bus costs

Washington reimburses districts through the STARS “Expected Allocation” formula. It pays for more riders, more served schools, and longer average routes — at the current margin roughly $2,650 per daily boarding-count per year and roughly $550k per served school.

§6Key findings

  1. Service expansion pays for itself under the funding formula. Shrinking middle-school walk zones to 1 mile adds ≈2,070 rides/day, zero buses, and ≈+$5.3M/yr net. Restoring high-school yellow bus adds ≈+$13.6M/yr net even at heavily discounted HS ridership.
  2. Closures cost transportation money from both directions. The 21-school Option A plan: +850 rides/day to carry (+$2.6M bus cost) and −$7.0M/yr in state funding — net ≈ −$9.6M/yr, an offset of roughly a third of its claimed $31.5M building savings. Option B nets ≈ −$7.5M/yr.
  3. Every consolidation receiver gains more riders than its closed partner had. Walkers at the closed school become bus riders at the receiver — e.g. Sanislo has ≈0 riders today, yet closing it adds ~74 rides/day at Highland Park.
  4. The bell-shift structure makes middle/high-school service nearly free in fleet terms while the elementary shift remains larger — the cheapest capacity in the system is on the MS/HS shift.
  5. Option-school conversions are roughly transportation-neutral (net ≈ −$0.4M/yr): lottery riders become walkers, but their replacement assignments scatter to non-walkable seats — and which five schools convert barely matters.
  6. Rider gains under closures skew toward higher-poverty schools — the consolidation receivers sit in poorer attendance areas than the schools being closed (see per-run equity cuts in the full reports).
Methods & caveats
  1. Full technical documentation, calibration and validation: OVERVIEW.md. Per-scenario detail (per-school movers, distance distributions, equity cuts): simulate/<scenario>/report.txt.
  2. High-school ridership is an uncalibrated assumption (SPS has never run HS yellow bus in the data): modeled as middle-school behavior ×0.7 for independent transit travel, with 11th–12th graders additionally ×0.5 for cars. Both factors are sampled, which is why HS intervals are wide.
  3. Funding deltas assume the EXAL formula’s coefficients hold and the prior-year cap never binds (true for every scenario shown), and apply modeled deltas to the official SY2024-25 formula inputs.
  4. Closure scenarios use the district’s named receivers where published (fab-4, Thurgood Marshall’s HCC) and distance-based defaults elsewhere — not official boundary redraws. Building-operations savings are out of scope.
  5. Bus cost uses the all-in vendor average ($148.9k/bus-yr); marginal route costs on an existing bus (driver-hours) are not priced, so “+0 bus” rows understate true marginal cost somewhat.
